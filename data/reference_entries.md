# Textbook reference entries

One entry for each of the thirty topics, recording what the PCTB textbook teaches at that grade: the definition as printed, the book's explanation of why the result holds, required vocabulary, worked examples, the grade boundary, and what counts as a minor or major error. Factual-accuracy labels were judged against these entries. Page references give the printed page, with the PDF page in parentheses where the two differ. References to D1 to D5 are to the rating dimensions in data/rubric.md. Grades 6 to 9 use the SNC 2022 / NCP 2023 editions; Grade 10 uses the preceding National Curriculum 2006 editions.

# PHYSICS

## P1 — Energy: forms and conversion *(Grade 6)*
**sci6 U8 Energy, p96 (101)**

**Scope:** energy as a physical quantity; potential vs kinetic energy; energy transfer demonstrated via a bouncing-ball energy-transfer diagram; the Law of Conservation of Energy and its application to different situations; renewable sources (wind, water, Sun, plants) vs non-renewable (coal, natural gas, crude oil); advantages of renewables.

**Vocabulary box (definitions as taught):** Energy = ability to do work · Kinetic energy = energy due to motion of an object · Potential energy = energy due to position of an object · Energy converter = a device which converts one form of energy into another · Solar panel = a device that converts solar energy into electricity.

**Forms named:** gravitational potential, elastic potential, kinetic, thermal, light, sound, electricity.

**Not errors:** no formulae (no ½mv², no mgh) — this grade is qualitative. STEAM tasks (solar panel/fan, solar water heater) are activities, not required content.

**Errors:** stating energy is created or destroyed (major) · defining potential energy as "stored energy" *only* without reference to position (minor) · confusing energy with force or power (major).

## P2 — Reflection of light and images in plane mirrors *(Grade 8)*
**sci8 U9 Reflection and Refraction of Light, p107 (112)**

**Scope:** basic properties of light (speed, transmission through media, absorption, reflection, dispersion); how an image is formed by a plane mirror; **the laws of reflection**; characteristics of the image formed by a plane mirror; distinguishing reflection from refraction with daily-life examples; apparent colour of objects from reflected/absorbed light; spherical mirrors (concave/convex) and their images; optical instruments using curved mirrors.

**Opening hooks the book uses:** why we see things in rooms sunlight doesn't reach directly; why a pencil appears bent in water; how a non-luminous Moon appears bright.

**Not errors:** no ray-optics calculations, no mirror formula, no magnification equation at this grade.

**Errors:** stating a plane-mirror image is real (major — it is virtual) · reversing the laws of reflection (major) · claiming a plane mirror magnifies (major).

## P3 — Sound waves: pitch, loudness, echo *(Grade 7)*
**sci7 U9 Waves and Energy, p103 (107)**

**Scope:** define a wave; mechanical vs electromagnetic waves with daily-life examples; longitudinal vs transverse; **sound identified as a mechanical wave, light as electromagnetic**; wavelength, frequency, time period; pitch related to frequency; amplitude related to loudness; factors affecting pitch and loudness; comparing waveforms; the inverse relation between time period and frequency; echo and hearing thunder after lightning.

**Vocabulary box:** Mechanical waves = require a material medium · Electromagnetic waves = do not require a medium · Wavelength = distance between peaks of two consecutive crests or troughs · Frequency = cycles per second passing a point · Time period = time for one complete wave cycle.

**Not errors:** no v = fλ calculations required; no anatomy of the ear beyond the hook question.

**Errors:** stating sound travels through vacuum (major) · linking pitch to amplitude or loudness to frequency (major — the book is explicit) · calling sound transverse (major).

## P4 — Pressure in liquids and Pascal's principle *(Grade 9)*
**phy9 §6.9 Pascal's Law, p139 (139); liquid pressure p133; hydraulic press p140; manometer p138**

**Statement as taught (verbatim sense):** when pressure is applied at one point in an **enclosed fluid**, it is transmitted equally to all parts of the fluid without loss.

**The book's "why":** balloon inflating uniformly; motorbike tyre inflating uniformly from one valve; then a flask-with-piston-and-side-tubes activity (a syringe on a pet bottle with drinking-straw side tubes as the low-cost version) — water rises to the same height in every side tube when the piston is pushed.

**Applications listed:** hydraulic press · car lift at service stations · hydraulic brakes. Stated advantages: liquids do not absorb the supplied energy; incompressibility lets them move heavy loads with great force.

**Also in scope:** a "Some Typical Pressures" table (atmospheric 1.013 × 10⁵ Pa, blood pressure 1.6 × 10⁴ Pa, motor tyre 2 × 10⁵ Pa, etc.).

**Errors:** omitting "enclosed" from the statement (minor, but material) · claiming pressure is transmitted only downward or in the direction applied (major) · saying liquids compress in a hydraulic system (major) · confusing Pascal's law with Archimedes' principle (major).

## P5 — Newton's three laws of motion *(Grade 9)*
**phy9 §3.4, p57–60 (57–60)**

**First law as taught (verbatim sense):** a body continues its state of rest, or of uniform motion in a straight line, unless acted upon by some external force. The book then derives a second definition of force from it: *force is an agency which changes or tends to change the state of rest or of uniform motion of a body*, glossed as "force causes acceleration."

**Inertia (explicit definition):** the property of a body to maintain its state of rest or of uniform motion in a straight line. **Mass is the measure of inertia** — greater mass, greater inertia (bicycle vs truck comparison). The first law is therefore also called the law of inertia.

**Canonical examples the book uses:** passengers bending forward when a bus stops suddenly and pushed back when it accelerates · a ball on a floor stopping because of friction, and the same object continuing forever in outer space · the tablecloth-pull demonstration.

**Preceding context:** §3.3 free-body diagrams (external forces: friction, gravity, normal force, drag, tension, applied force; object drawn as a box with arrows outward; arrow length = magnitude).

**Not errors:** at this grade the second law appears as F = ma; no momentum-form derivation required (though momentum p69 and conservation p72 exist separately).

**Errors:** stating a force is needed to keep an object moving at constant velocity (major — this is the exact misconception the book targets) · defining inertia as a force (major) · confusing mass and weight (major; weight covered p62).

## P6 — Distance vs displacement, speed vs velocity *(Grade 9)*
**phy9 §2.4–2.5, p34 (34); position p29; uniform/variable velocity p36; uniform acceleration p37; speed–time graph p41**

**Definitions as taught:** distance = the length of the actual path of the motion (scalar). Displacement = a **vector** whose magnitude is the shortest distance between the initial and final positions, with direction from initial to final position; also framed as "the change in position." Speed = distance ÷ time, v = S/t, so S = vt (eq. 2.1).

**The book's canonical example:** travelling Lahore → Multan by car, speedometer reads 320 km — that is the distance, and it is not the shortest path because the car took many turns. Then a car moving A → B along a curved path: distance = length of the curve, displacement **d** = straight line AB.

**Brain teaser the book poses:** a car on a circular road may have constant speed but its velocity changes at every instant. (Good discriminator for explanation quality.)

**Also in scope:** scalar vs vector (p29), types of motion — translatory, rotatory, vibratory, linear, random (p32–33), uniform vs variable velocity, uniform acceleration, speed–time graphs and slope.

**Errors:** treating distance and displacement as interchangeable (major) · omitting direction from velocity/displacement (major) · stating displacement is always less than distance (minor — equal when motion is along a straight line without reversal).

## P7 — Ohm's law and simple electric circuits *(Grade 10)*
**phy10 Unit 14 Current Electricity, §14.4**

**Statement as taught (verbatim sense):** the amount of current passing through a conductor is directly proportional to the potential difference applied across its ends, **provided the temperature and the physical state of the conductor do not change**. I ∝ V, hence V = IR.

**Resistance (definition as taught):** the property of a substance which offers opposition to the flow of current through it. The book's mechanism: this opposition comes from the **collisions of moving electrons with the atoms of the substance**. Unit: when a potential difference of one volt is applied and one ampere flows, resistance is one ohm.

**Ohmic vs non-ohmic (explicitly in scope, and the book's stated *limitation* of Ohm's law):** materials with constant resistance over a wide range of voltages are **ohmic** (most metals — straight-line V–I graph). **Non-ohmic:** a **filament lamp** (resistance rises as it heats, so the V–I graph curves) and a **thermistor** (resistance *falls* as it heats). The book asks students to interpret all three V–I graphs.

**Also in scope:** current I = Q/t and the ampere · conventional vs electronic current · potential difference and e.m.f. (and the distinction between them) · galvanometer, ammeter (series), voltmeter (parallel) · R = ρL/A and specific resistance · conductors vs insulators · series (Re = R₁+R₂+R₃) and parallel (1/Re = 1/R₁+1/R₂+1/R₃) combinations · Joule's law W = I²Rt = V²t/R · power P = IV = I²R · kilowatt-hour and cost of electricity · D.C. vs A.C. · house wiring, live/neutral/earth, fuse, circuit breaker.

**Canonical examples the book uses:** heating element at 60 V drawing 2 A → R = 30 Ω · 6 kΩ and 4 kΩ in series across 10 V · the water-in-a-pipe analogy for resistance (short/wide pipe vs long/narrow pipe).

**Not errors:** no Kirchhoff's laws at this grade; internal resistance appears only incidentally in one numerical problem.

**Errors:** stating current is proportional to resistance (major) · omitting the constant-temperature proviso when *stating* the law (minor, but the book prints it as part of the statement) · claiming a filament lamp obeys Ohm's law (major — it is the book's standard counter-example) · adding resistances directly in a parallel circuit (major) · treating e.m.f. and potential difference as the same quantity (minor).

## P8 — Electromagnetic induction *(Grade 10)*
**phy10 Unit 15 Electromagnetism, §15.5–15.6**

**Definition as taught (verbatim sense):** the process of generating an induced current in a circuit by **changing the number of magnetic lines of force passing through it**.

**Faraday's law as taught:** the induced e.m.f. is directly proportional to the **rate of change of the number of magnetic lines of force** through the circuit. *(Note for coding: the book works in "number of lines of force", not "magnetic flux". A model that answers in flux language is not wrong, but the grade convention is lines of force — useful discriminator for D2.)*

**Factors affecting the size of induced e.m.f. (the book lists exactly two):** the speed of relative motion between coil and magnet, and the number of turns of the coil.

**Lenz's law as taught:** the direction of an induced current is always such that it **opposes the cause that produces it**. The book explicitly frames this as a consequence of **conservation of energy** — the mechanical energy of the hand pushing the magnet becomes the electrical energy in the coil.

**The book's canonical demonstrations:** a wire loop moved *down* through a U-magnet's field deflects the galvanometer one way, moved *up* deflects it the other way, and **held stationary or moved parallel to the field gives no deflection at all** · a bar magnet pushed into and pulled out of a solenoid, with opposite deflections.

**Also in scope:** magnetic field around a current-carrying conductor, right-hand grip rule · force on a current-carrying conductor and Fleming's left-hand rule · torque on a current-carrying coil and the D.C. motor (with split-ring commutator) · A.C. generator and its e.m.f.–time graph · mutual induction · transformer, Vs/Vp = Ns/Np, step-up vs step-down · high-voltage transmission · relay.

**Errors:** stating that a magnet held stationary inside a coil induces a current (major — the book's experiment is built to disprove exactly this) · stating the induced current *aids* rather than opposes the change (major) · swapping the energy conversions of motor and generator (major — motor: electrical → mechanical; generator: mechanical → electrical) · stating a transformer works on D.C. (major — the book is explicit).

## P9 — Refraction and image formation by convex lenses *(Grade 10)*
**phy10 Unit 12 Geometrical Optics, §12.4 and §12.8–12.10**

**Refraction as taught:** the bending of light as it passes from one transparent medium into another. **Laws of refraction:** (i) incident ray, refracted ray and normal at the point of incidence lie in the same plane; (ii) sin i / sin r = constant = n (**Snell's law**).

**Refractive index:** n = speed of light in air ÷ speed of light in the medium. Table values the book gives: air 1.00 · ice 1.31 · water 1.33 · ethyl alcohol 1.36 · crown glass 1.52 · flint glass 1.66 · cubic zirconia 2.21 · diamond 2.42.

**Convex lens (definition as taught):** the lens which causes incident parallel rays to converge at a point; **thick at the centre, thin at the edges**. Terms required: principal axis, optical centre, principal focus, focal length. **Power of a lens** P = 1/f (in metres), unit **dioptre** (D); positive for convex, negative for concave.

**The three principal rays the book uses for ray diagrams:** (1) a ray parallel to the principal axis passes through the focal point after refraction; (2) a ray through the optical centre passes undeviated; (3) a ray through the focal point emerges parallel to the principal axis.

**The five image cases (this is the grade standard and the highest-signal part of the topic):**
| Object position | Image |
|---|---|
| Beyond 2F | between F and 2F · real · inverted · smaller |
| At 2F | at 2F · real · inverted · same size |
| Between F and 2F | beyond 2F · real · inverted · larger |
| At F | **no image** — refracted rays are parallel and never meet |
| Between lens and F | same side as object · **virtual · erect · larger** (the magnifying-glass case) |

**Lens formula:** 1/f = 1/p + 1/q, with sign conventions printed in the book (f positive for converging, negative for diverging; q negative for a virtual image). Magnification m = image height ÷ object height.

**Also in scope:** total internal reflection and critical angle · optical fibre, light pipe, endoscope · refraction through a prism and angle of deviation · camera, slide projector, photograph enlarger · simple and compound microscope, refracting telescope · the human eye, accommodation, myopia and hypermetropia.

**Not errors:** no lens-maker's formula; no algebraic derivation of the lens formula at this grade.

**Errors:** stating a convex lens always forms a real image (major — the object-inside-F case is explicitly virtual, erect and magnified) · saying light bends *away* from the normal when entering a denser medium (major) · inverting the refractive index definition (major) · stating that the colour or frequency of light changes on refraction (major — the book's Physics Insight says speed and wavelength change but frequency and hence colour do not).

## P10 — Radioactivity: alpha, beta and gamma radiation *(Grade 10)*
**phy10 Unit 18 Atomic and Nuclear Physics, §18.2–18.5**

**Definition as taught:** the **spontaneous** emission of radiation by unstable nuclei is called natural radioactivity.

**The three radiations as taught:** **alpha** = a helium nucleus, two protons and two neutrons, charge +2e · **beta** = a stream of high-energy electrons · **gamma** = electromagnetic radiation of very short wavelength (high-frequency photons) emitted by an excited nucleus. In a magnetic field, alpha and beta bend in **opposite** directions and gamma is **undeflected**.

**Ionising power (order as taught):** alpha > beta > gamma. The book's stated reason: alpha has **large positive charge and large mass**.

**Penetrating power (order as taught):** gamma > beta > alpha. Alpha is stopped by paper or thin aluminium foil and travels only a few cm in air; beta penetrates several mm of aluminium and metres of air; gamma penetrates roughly 30 cm of lead or 2 km of air.

**Decay equations as taught:**
- Alpha: ᴬZX → ᴬ⁻⁴Z₋₂Y + ⁴₂He · book's example ²²⁶₈₈Ra → ²²²₈₆Rn + ⁴₂He
- Beta: ᴬZX → ᴬZ₊₁Y + ⁰₋₁e · book's example ¹⁴₆C → ¹⁴₇N + ⁰₋₁e
- Gamma: A and Z **both unchanged** · book's example ⁶⁰₂₇Co → ⁶⁰₂₇Co + γ

**Half-life:** the time during which half of the unstable nuclei disintegrate. Remaining nuclei after t half-lives = N₀ × (1/2)ᵗ. Book's anchor values: radium-226 = 1620 years; carbon-14 = 5730 years.

**Explicit statement the book makes (high-signal):** radioactivity **does not depend on chemical combination or reaction, and is not affected by temperature, pressure, or electric or magnetic fields**.

**Also in scope:** atomic number Z, mass number A, nuclide notation ᴬZX, isotopes (protium, deuterium, tritium) · background radiation and its sources (the book's breakdown: 57% rocks and soil, 21% cosmic rays, 20% radon, 2% fallout) · radioisotopes and uses (iodine-131 for thyroid, phosphorus-32 for brain tumours and for tracing fertiliser uptake, cobalt-60 for tumours, carbon dating) · fission and fusion · hazards and safety precautions.

**Errors:** stating alpha is more penetrating than gamma (major — this is the classic misconception and the book is explicit) · stating beta emission *decreases* the atomic number (major — it increases by 1) · stating gamma emission changes A or Z (major) · claiming radioactive decay is speeded up by heat or by chemical reaction (major — directly contradicts the book) · stating that a sample is completely decayed after two half-lives (major).

# CHEMISTRY & BIOLOGY

## C1 — Photosynthesis *(Grade 7)*
**sci7 U1 Plant Systems, p1 (5)**

**Scope:** root and shoot systems; internal/external structure of leaf, stem, root; role of **xylem and phloem** in transport of water and food; **define photosynthesis and derive the word equation for it**; leaf structure adapted to photosynthesis; plants need minerals (limited to magnesium for chlorophyll, nitrates for proteins); respiration and its word equation; **compare and contrast photosynthesis with respiration**; transpiration and factors affecting it.

**Vocabulary box:** Chlorophyll = green pigment in chloroplast which absorbs light · Stomata = tiny openings in leaves for gaseous exchange and removal of extra water · Mesophyll cells = cells rich in chloroplast, specialised for food preparation · Vascular bundles = xylem + phloem system · Root hairs · Transpiration.

**Required output:** the **word equation** (not the balanced symbol equation) is the grade standard.

**Not errors:** omitting light-dependent/light-independent stages, ATP, or Calvin cycle — all beyond this grade.

**Errors:** stating plants take in oxygen and release carbon dioxide as their food-making process (major) · placing photosynthesis in the roots (major) · omitting light or chlorophyll from the requirements (major).

## C2 — States of matter and changes of state *(Grade 6)*
**sci6 U5 Matter as Particles, p52 (57)**

**Scope:** the **Particle Theory of Matter**; particle model for movement and arrangement in the three states; why gases and liquids take the shape of their container but solids do not; why liquids and gases flow easily; evidence for the existence of particles from daily life (adding air to a basketball, compressing air in a syringe, dissolving sugar, evaporating salt water); diffusion explained by particle theory; **changes of state: melting, freezing, evaporation, condensation, sublimation** explained using the particle model.

**Vocabulary box (definitions as taught — note these are the grade's exact standard):** Melting = solid → liquid on heating · Freezing = liquid → solid on cooling · **Evaporation = liquid → gas at any temperature** · **Boiling = bubbling of a liquid into its gaseous state on heating** · Condensation = gas → liquid on cooling · Sublimation = solid directly → gas without passing through liquid.

**Errors:** conflating evaporation and boiling (major — the book distinguishes them precisely: evaporation at *any* temperature) · stating particles themselves expand on heating (major) · omitting the particle model entirely and describing only observations (this is pedagogical flattening, score low on D3 rather than an accuracy error).

## C3 — Plant cell vs animal cell *(Grade 6)*
**sci6 U1 Cellular Organization, p1 (6)**

**Scope:** cells as the basic unit of life; the hierarchy cell → tissue → organ → organ system → organism; structures of common cells (nerve, muscle, epithelium, blood) related to function; **structures in animal and plant cells as seen under a simple microscope — explicitly limited to cell membrane, cytoplasm, nucleus, cell wall, chloroplast and vacuole (plus mitochondria in the vocabulary box)**; similarities and differences between plant and animal cells; sketching and labelling both; onion-peel and cheek-cell slides.

**Vocabulary box:** Cell = the basic unit of life · Tissue = organization of similar cells · Organ = grouping of related tissues · Organ system = coordination of different organs to complete the task · Organism = association of different organ systems · Mitochondria = organelles related to produce energy from food · Chloroplast = organelles which store light energy in the form of food · Epidermis · Xylem · Phloem.

**Grade boundary (important):** the SLO explicitly caps the organelle list. Ribosomes, ER, Golgi, lysosomes are **out of scope** — mentioning them is not a factual error but is a grade-appropriateness (D2) problem.

**Errors:** giving animal cells a cell wall or chloroplasts (major) · stating plant cells lack mitochondria (major) · describing the nucleus as producing energy (major).

## C4 — Acids, bases and neutralisation *(Grade 8)*
**sci8 U7 Acids, Bases and Salts, p76 (81)**

**Scope:** classify acids, alkalis and salts with examples; physical properties of each; **define pH and its ranges with reference to indicators**; interpret the pH scale, identify acids and alkalis on it, and **describe neutralisation with real-life examples**; uses in daily life.

**The book's pH bands (printed on the chapter opener — this is the grade standard):** strong acids pH 0–2 · weak acids pH 3–6 · neutral pH 7 · weak alkalis pH 8–11 · strong alkalis pH 12–14.

**Definition as taught:** acids are compounds which produce **hydrogen ions (H⁺)** in their aqueous solutions. Word origin given: Latin *acidus*, "sour". Examples used: citric acid in citrus fruits; HCl in gastric juice, acting as antiseptic and aiding protein digestion.

**Hooks the book uses:** where stomach HCl comes from; why wood ash is used to wash utensils; which salts are in drinking water.

**Not errors:** no Brønsted–Lowry or Lewis definitions; no pH = −log[H⁺] calculation at this grade.

**Errors:** reversing the pH scale direction (major) · stating neutralisation produces only water (major — salt + water) · describing all acids as dangerous/corrosive without qualification (minor).

## C5 — The human digestive system *(Grade 6)*
**sci6 U4 Human Digestive System, p42 (47)**

**Scope:** importance of digestion; **physical and chemical digestion**; sequence the main regions of the **alimentary canal** and associated organs and describe their functions; **briefly describe the role of enzymes in digestion**; blood transports the products of digestion and undigested products are egested/defecated; brief description of major digestive disorders.

**Vocabulary box:** Glands = organs which secrete enzymes and hormones · Enzyme = types of proteins that speed up chemical reactions occurring in our body · Salivary glands = secrete saliva containing enzymes that start digestion in the oral cavity · Gastric glands = in the stomach walls, secrete gastric juice · Bile = fluid released by liver, stored in gallbladder, helps digestion of fats · Pancreatic juice = released by pancreas, contains enzymes that complete digestion in the small intestine.

**Note:** the chapter opener already shows an enzyme lock-and-key-style diagram (enzyme combines with substance, molecule breaks, enzyme free to take part in next reaction) — so enzyme *reusability* is in scope even at Grade 6.

**Errors:** stating most absorption occurs in the stomach (major — small intestine) · saying bile is produced by the gallbladder (major — produced by liver, stored in gallbladder) · describing enzymes as being used up (major, and directly contradicts the book's own diagram).

## C6 — Atomic structure: protons, neutrons, electrons *(Grade 7)*
**sci7 U4 Structure of an Atom, p44 (48)**

**Scope:** describe and draw atomic structure in terms of electrons, protons and neutrons; **explain how an atom is electrically neutral**; differentiate atomic number from mass number; determine both from particle counts; arrangement of electrons in **K, L and M shells**; draw atomic structures of the **first eighteen elements**; the periodic table as systematic organisation; recognise periods and groups.

**Vocabulary box:** Electron = negatively charged particle found *around* the nucleus · Proton = positively charged particle *in* the nucleus · Neutron = neutral particle *in* the nucleus · Atomic number = number of protons · Mass number = total number of protons and neutrons · Isotopes = atoms of the same element having different mass numbers.

**Shell notation:** K, L, M (not n = 1, 2, 3) is the grade convention. Chapter-opener diagrams show Na (P=11, N=12) and Cl (P=17, N=18).

**Errors:** placing electrons in the nucleus (major) · defining atomic number as protons + neutrons (major) · stating isotopes differ in proton number (major) · using 2-8-8 without shell names is acceptable, but subshells (s, p, d) are out of scope (D2 issue, not an error).

## C7 — The periodic table: groups and periods *(Grade 8)*
**sci8 U5 Periodic Table, p46 (51)**

**Scope:** recognise the periodic table as a way of classifying elements into **groups and periods**; **identify the names and location of the first 18 elements only**; identify properties of metals and non-metals; relate those properties to the uses of metals.

**Facts stated in the text:** an element cannot be split into two or more substances by ordinary chemical changes; the basic particle of an element is an atom; atoms of one element are alike and differ from those of other elements; **about 118 elements, 92 of which occur naturally**.

**Grade boundary (important):** the SLO caps element knowledge at the **first 18**. Discussion of transition metals, lanthanides, actinides, or f-block is beyond grade (D2 issue). Alloys appear only as an opening hook.

**Errors:** describing groups as horizontal rows or periods as vertical columns (major) · stating group number always equals the number of shells (major — that is the period) · claiming all elements are naturally occurring (major).

## C8 — Ionic vs covalent bonding *(Grade 9)*
**chem9 Ch3 Chemical Bonding, p32–51 (36–55)**

**Definitions the SLOs require verbatim in substance:**
- **Ionic bond** = strong electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions.
- **Covalent bond** = strong electrostatic attraction between shared electrons and two nuclei.
- **Metallic bond** = strong electrostatic attraction between a cloud/sea of delocalised electrons and positively charged cations.

**Also in scope:** noble-gas electronic configuration, **octet and duplet rules**; formation of cations vs anions; electropositive/electronegative nature of metals and non-metals; **coordinate covalent bond** (also required); properties of compounds explained by bonding and structure; strength of forces of attraction and its effect on **melting and boiling points**; availability of free charged particles explaining electrical conduction in ionic compounds (solid and molten), covalent compounds and metals; substances that ionise in water (acids dissolving and conducting); suitability of graphite (lubricant, electrode), diamond (cutting tools) and metals (wires, sheets) for industrial use.

**Required drawings:** dot-and-cross diagrams and Lewis structures for NaBr, NaF, CaCl₂ and for H₂, Cl₂, O₂, N₂, H₂O, CH₄, NH₃, HCl, CH₃OH, C₂H₄, CO₂, HCN.

**Errors:** describing an ionic bond as electron *sharing* (major) · stating ionic solids conduct electricity in the solid state (major — molten or aqueous only) · omitting the duplet rule for H/He when relevant (minor) · calling a covalent bond weak (major — the SLO says "strong").

## C9 — Enzymes and how they speed up reactions *(Grade 9)*
**bio9 Ch7 Enzymes, p105–113 (105–113). §7.2 Enzymes p110, §7.3 Mechanism p112**

**Definition as taught (verbatim sense):** enzymes are **biological catalysts** that speed up chemical reactions in living organisms **without being consumed in the process**. They are primarily proteins and are **highly specific to their substrates** (the molecules that undergo enzyme-controlled reactions). Most enzymes speed reactions **millions of times** faster than uncatalysed reactions.

**Characteristics named:** chemical nature — predominantly proteins, typically 100 to 1,000 amino acids · globular three-dimensional structure. **Side note in the book:** some RNA molecules also act as enzymes, called **ribozymes**, found primarily in ribosomes and in specific viruses and bacteria.

**Mechanism (§7.3):** an enzyme has one or more **pockets or clefts** on its surface called **active sites**, directly involved in catalysis. **Two models** are presented — the book explicitly says two. **Lock and Key model**, proposed by **Emil Fischer in 1894**: the active site has a **fixed** structure; the substrate fits precisely into it forming an **enzyme–substrate complex**; the enzyme catalyses the reaction, substrate is transformed into product(s), then product is released. (Induced fit is the second model, immediately following.)

**Metabolic context (§7.1):** catabolism (e.g. lipolysis: lipid → fatty acids + glycerol) vs anabolism (photosynthesis; protein synthesis from amino acids).

**Applications the book uses:** biological detergents (protease for protein stains, amylase in dish washing for starch residues) · fermentation industry (enzymes degrade starch and proteins to simple sugars, amino acids and peptides for microorganisms such as yeast).

**Also in scope:** §7.4 factors affecting enzyme activity (p109/113) · §7.5 enzyme inhibition (p111/115).

**Errors:** stating enzymes are consumed or used up (major) · stating enzymes are all proteins without qualification (minor — the book flags ribozymes) · attributing lock-and-key to the wrong scientist or omitting that two models exist (minor) · claiming enzymes change the equilibrium rather than the rate (major).

## C10 — Mendelian inheritance and Punnett squares *(Grade 10)*
**bio10 Chapter 15 Inheritance**

**Framing the book uses:** genetics is the branch of biology in which inheritance is studied; inheritance is the transmission of characteristics (**traits**) from parents to offspring, via **genes** carried on chromosomes.

**Vocabulary required at this grade:** gene · **locus** · **allele** (alternative form of a gene) · **genotype**, and its two types **homozygous** (two identical alleles) and **heterozygous** (two different alleles) · **dominant** and **recessive** alleles · **phenotype** (the expression of the genotype as a trait) · P₁, F₁ and F₂ generations.

**Why Mendel chose the pea plant — the book lists his own four reasons:** a number of different traits available to study; **contrasting** forms of each trait; normally self-fertilising but cross-fertilisation is possible; a short, fast life cycle.

**Law of Segregation (monohybrid cross).** Round × wrinkled seeds → F₁ all round → F₂ **5474 round : 1850 wrinkled ≈ 3 : 1**. Tall × short → F₁ all tall → F₂ 3 : 1. Conclusion as stated: traits are controlled by discrete factors (genes) present **in pairs**; during gamete formation the two alleles **segregate** so each gamete receives one; on fertilisation the offspring again receives the genes in pairs.

**Law of Independent Assortment (dihybrid cross).** RRYY × rryy → F₁ all round yellow → F₂ **315 : 108 : 101 : 32 ≈ 9 : 3 : 3 : 1**. Conclusion as stated: the alleles of one gene pair segregate **independently** of the alleles of other gene pairs.

**Punnett square (definition as taught):** a diagram used to predict the outcome of a particular cross, named after **R. C. Punnett**. All possible gametes of one parent are crossed against all possible gametes of the other on a checkerboard.

**Co-dominance — the book's example is ABO blood groups.** Gene I with three alleles: **IᴬIᴬ or IᴬI → antigen A → group A** · **IᴮIᴮ or IᴮI → antigen B → group B** · **II → no antigen → group O** · **IᴬIᴮ → both antigens → group AB**. Iᴬ and Iᴮ are each dominant over i, but **co-dominant with each other** — both express fully, neither masks the other.

**Incomplete dominance — the book's example is the Four O'Clock plant.** RR = red, rr = white, **Rr = pink** (a blend; neither allele dominant). Crossing two pink plants gives F₂ **1 red : 2 pink : 1 white**. The book notes there is no separate gene "for pink".

**Grade boundary (important for D2):** no chi-square or statistical testing of ratios · no linkage or quantitative crossing-over problems · no sex-linked inheritance. Crossing over is mentioned only in passing as a source of variation.

**Errors:** giving the monohybrid F₂ ratio as anything other than 3 : 1, or the dihybrid F₂ ratio as anything other than 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 (major) · describing the pink Four O'Clock flower as co-dominance, or blood group AB as incomplete dominance (major — the book draws this distinction explicitly and it is the single most likely confusion) · stating that alleles blend permanently and cannot separate in later generations (major — directly contradicts segregation, and the F₂ reappearance of red and white disproves it) · using genotype and phenotype interchangeably (major) · stating that a dominant allele alters or destroys the recessive allele (major — the book states it only suppresses its *expression*).

## M1 — Why is the product of two negative numbers positive? *(Grade 6)*
**math6 Sub-Domain 3 Laws of Integers, p27 (30); multiplication with sign rules p35 (38)**

**Rule as taught (Remember box, verbatim):** (+) × (+) = + · (+) × (−) = − · (−) × (+) = − · **(−) × (−) = +**

**The book's "why" — this is the key entry for this topic.** PCTB does **not** simply state the rule. It explains multiplication of integers as **jumps on a number line**, where a negative multiplier reverses direction:
- (−6) × (−2) is explained as "six jumps, each jump is two steps **in the opposite direction of −6**", landing on +12.
- Contrast (−3) × (+6): "three jumps, each of six steps **in the same direction** of (−3)" → −18.
- And (+5) × (−3): "five jumps, each of three steps in the same direction of (−3)" → −15.

**Why this matters for the study:** the curriculum standard here is an *explanation* (direction reversal on a number line), not just the sign rule. A model that only recites "negative times negative equals positive" is **pedagogically below the textbook standard** — score low on D3, though it is not a factual error. This is one of the highest-signal topics in the set.

**Also in scope:** addition/subtraction of like and unlike integers on a number line; commutative, associative and distributive laws; division of integers up to 2 digits. Like integers = same signs; unlike integers = different signs.

**Errors:** giving the wrong sign in any of the four cases (major) · stating multiplication of two negatives gives a negative (major) · explaining via "two negatives cancel like in English grammar" as the *only* justification (not an error, but pedagogically flat vs the book).

## M2 — Ratio and rate in everyday problems *(Grade 6)*
**math6 Sub-Domain 4 Rate, Ratio and Percentage, p49 (52)**

**Definition as taught (verbatim sense):** a ratio is a **numerical comparison between two quantities or things of the same kind**.

**Three forms required:** word form ("4 to 5"), ratio form (4 : 5), fraction form (4/5). Read as "the ratio of 4 to 5"; generally x : y, "the ratio of x to y". More than two quantities written x : y : z.

**Remember box (a real constraint):** always compare quantities of the **same kind with the same unit**, because the ratio of two like quantities can be defined but the ratio of two different quantities is meaningless — the book's own example: you cannot compare a person's weight with their height.

**Canonical examples the book uses:** four green mangoes to five yellow mangoes (4 : 5); eggs distributed across plates (12 eggs in two plates vs 18 in three); **the Pakistan national flag, height : length = 2 : 3**.

**SLOs:** define and calculate ratio, equivalent ratio, rate and percentage; solve real-life problems involving ratio, rate and percentage.

**Errors:** forming a ratio between unlike quantities without comment (major — explicitly contradicts the book) · writing a ratio in the wrong order relative to the stated comparison (major) · confusing ratio with fraction-of-the-whole (minor unless the answer depends on it).

## M4 — Solving linear equations in one variable *(Grade 7)*

A **linear equation in one variable** has only one variable of degree 1; standard form **ax + b = 0**, where a and b are real numbers, **a ≠ 0**, and x is the variable (examples: 5x = 7, 10 − 3x = 7, 5y + 10 = 5(y + 2)).

## M5 — Pythagoras theorem and why it works *(Grade 8)*
**math8 Domain 3 Measurement, Mensuration §3.1, p131 (134)**

**Statement as taught:** in right-angled triangle ABC with ∠C = 90° and a, b, c opposite ∠A, ∠B, ∠C: **a² + b² = c²**, presented as (Base)² + (Altitude)² = (Hypotenuse)².

**The book's "why":** an **informal** cut-and-place proof done as a hands-on activity — draw a right triangle with sides in ratio **3 : 4 : 5**, construct squares on all three sides, cut the smaller squares into strips, and place them to exactly cover the square on the hypotenuse. Conclusion stated: the area of the square on side c equals the total area of the squares on a and b. The book explicitly calls this an *informal proof*; no algebraic or formal geometric proof is given.

**Required vocabulary (Remember box):** hypotenuse = side opposite the right angle · base = adjacent horizontal side · altitude = vertical side.

**Canonical applications:** finding the third side given two; real-life distance problems (the book's opener: distance between two houses); historical framing — Egyptians measuring the width of the Nile with a chain triangle in ratio 3 : 4 : 5, which the book says prompted Pythagoras ~2500 years ago.

**Grade boundaries:** no converse required; no formal proof expected; irrational answers as decimals are fine.

**Errors:** applying a² + b² = c² with c not the hypotenuse (major) · stating it holds for non-right triangles (major) · calling the strip-cutting proof formal, or misdescribing which square gets covered (minor).

## M10 — Probability of simple events *(Grade 8)*
**math8 Domain 5 Statistics and Probability (ii) Probability, p206 (209)**

**Scope (full SLO list):** determine that if the probability of an event occurring is **P**, the probability of it **not** occurring is **1 − P**; perform probability experiments (tossing a coin, rolling a die, spinning a spinner) a certain number of times to estimate the probability of a simple event; **compare experimental and theoretical probability** in simple events; explain and compute the probability of **mutually exclusive, independent, and equally likely** events; calculate combining probabilities with **"AND" and "OR"**; predict outcomes of simple combined events using **sample space** and **tree diagrams**; calculate probability of simple combined events; apply probability to real-life situations.

## M7 — Factorising quadratic expressions *(Grade 9)*
**math9 U4 Factorization and Algebraic Manipulation, p65–81. Algebra tiles §4.1 p65–67; middle-term breaking §4.1.3 p67–69; higher types p70–73; HCF/LCM §4.3 p74–77; square roots §4.4 p77–79; real-world problems §4.4.1 p78–79**

**Unit SLOs:** identify common factors and trinomial factoring **concretely, pictorially and symbolically**; factorise quadratic and cubic expressions of the listed types (x² + px + q · ax² + bx + c · a⁴ + a²b² + b⁴ · a⁴ + b⁴ · (x+a)(x+b)(x+c)(x+d) + k and + kx² variants · (ax²+bx+c)(ax²+bx+d) + k · a³ ± 3a²b + 3ab² ± b³ · a³ ± b³); HCF and LCM of algebraic expressions and LCM × HCF = product of the two polynomials; square root of an algebraic expression by factorisation and division; apply factorisation to real-world problems (engineering, physics, finance).

**Topic boundary:** the study topic is *quadratic* factorisation, so the grade standard for our prompts is §4.1 (common factors, algebra tiles) plus Type I middle-term breaking. Quartic/cubic types, HCF/LCM and square roots sit in the same unit but beyond the topic label — omitting them is NOT an error and NOT a D2 problem.

**Definitions as taught:** a common factor is "an expression that divides two or more expressions exactly" (2x − 6 = 2(x − 3)). Trinomial factoring = converting a trinomial into a product of two binomials; trinomial = three terms, binomial = two terms. Remember box (verbatim): **an expression having degree 2 is called a quadratic expression**.

**The book's "why" — algebra tiles (concrete → pictorial → symbolic):** grey tiles represent +1, +x, +x²; white tiles −1, −x, −x²; a grey/white pair is a **zero pair**. A trinomial is arranged into a rectangle and **the factors are the lengths of its sides**. Worked: x² + 2x = x(x + 2) · x² − 5x + 4 = (x − 1)(x − 4) · x² − 3x − 10 = (x + 2)(x − 5), where the rectangle only closes after adding an x/−x zero pair "because 2x − 2x = 0". This area-model justification is the curriculum's explanation of *why* factorisation works. A model that only states the product/sum trick with no structural justification is below the book's standard on D3 (pedagogical flattening, not a factual error).

**Canonical method — middle-term breaking:** find two numbers whose **product equals the constant term** (or a × c when a ≠ 1) **and whose sum equals the middle coefficient**, using the book's product-of-factors / sum-of-factors tables. Worked: x² + 9x + 14 = (x + 2)(x + 7) · x² − 11x + 24 = (x − 8)(x − 3) · p² + 11p + 18 = (p + 9)(p + 2) · the a ≠ 1 case as six labelled steps for 2x² + 17x + 26 = (x + 2)(2x + 13) · 3x² − 4x − 4 = (3x + 2)(x − 2).

**Real-world framing the unit itself uses (fair game for examples):** minimum-cost quadratic C(x) = 5x² − 25x + 30 factorised as 5(x − 2)(x − 3); zero-return and break-even problems; beam deflection Y(x) = 2x² − 8x + 6.

**Not errors:** no quadratic formula and no completing the square at this grade (Grade 10 / M8 territory) — solving by formula instead of factorising is off-standard for the prompt (D2/D3 issue), not a factual error · restricting to integer-factorable examples mirrors the book.

**Errors:** wrong sign in a factor pair, or factors that do not expand back to the original expression (major) · reversing the product/sum roles — choosing numbers that *sum* to the constant and *multiply* to the middle coefficient (major) · presenting the roots x = 2, 3 as "the factorisation" in place of the factors (major if it replaces the factors; minor alongside them) · misidentifying degree — calling a cubic quadratic or vice versa (major) · claiming every quadratic factorises over the integers (minor, unless the explanation depends on it).

## M9 — Trigonometric ratios in right-angled triangles *(Grade 9)*
**math9 U6 Trigonometry, p97–122. Ratios defined §6.2.1 p105; complementary angles §6.2.2 p106; identities §6.3 p107–109; special angles §6.4 p111–113; solution of right triangles §6.5 p113–116; elevation/depression §6.6 p118–119**

**Unit SLOs:** identify angles in standard position in degrees and radians; apply Pythagoras theorem and the sine, cosine and tangent ratios **for acute angles of a right-angled triangle**; solve real-life 2-D problems involving angles of elevation and depression; prove trigonometric identities and apply them; solve real-life problems involving the identities.

**Side-naming convention (the grade standard):** in right-angled triangle ACB with θ = m∠CAB and m∠ACB = 90°, side BC is the **perpendicular** (opposite θ), AC the **base**, AB the **hypotenuse**; mBC = a, mAC = b, mAB = c. The six ratios as defined: sin θ = Perpendicular/Hypotenuse = a/c · cos θ = Base/Hypotenuse = b/c · tan θ = Perpendicular/Base = a/b · cosec, sec, cot as the respective reciprocals, with note boxes cosec θ = 1/sin θ, sec θ = 1/cos θ, cot θ = 1/tan θ, and the derivations tan θ = sin θ/cos θ, cot θ = cos θ/sin θ.

**Terminology note (D4):** the book says **Perpendicular / Base / Hypotenuse**, not "opposite/adjacent". International opposite/adjacent wording is not a factual error, but P/B/H is the established local convention — relevant to Urdu terminology scoring as well (expected Urdu terms عمود / قاعدہ / وتر; teacher to confirm during label validation).

**Special angles (§6.4) — derived, not just tabulated:** 45° from the diagonal of a unit square (c = √2); 30° and 60° from an equilateral triangle of side 2 bisected (height √3). Standard value table for 0°, 30°, 45°, 60°, 90°; **the book's table prints tan 90° = ∞** (a model writing "undefined" is fine and not an error).

**Complementary angles:** sin(90° − θ) = cos θ, cos(90° − θ) = sin θ, tan(90° − θ) = cot θ, and the sec/cosec/cot counterparts.

**Identities (§6.3):** derived from a² + b² = c² by dividing through by c², b², a²: sin²θ + cos²θ = 1 · 1 + tan²θ = sec²θ · 1 + cot²θ = cosec²θ — named "Pythagoras identities", illustrated with the 3-4-5 squares figure.

**Canonical applications:** solving right triangles in four cases (one side + one angle · hypotenuse + angle · two sides · one side + hypotenuse) · angle of elevation = from horizontal eye level *up* to the top of an object; angle of depression = *down* to its foot · worked: pole 40 m high, elevation 60° → distance 40/√3 ≈ 23.09 m; tower 30 m, depression 45° → man is 30 m away · exercise setups: flag post, ladder against wall, lighthouse and ship, kite on 120 m string.

**Topic boundary:** the topic label is the *ratios*. Radian conversion, arc length/sector area (§6.1), the identities and elevation/depression are same-unit extensions — omitting them is not an error. Using an elevation/depression scenario as the "simple example" is squarely on-standard.

**Not errors:** restricting the ratios to acute angles of right triangles (the book does exactly this, merely noting the concepts extend beyond 90°) · opposite/adjacent instead of perpendicular/base · tan 90° as "undefined" rather than ∞.

**Errors:** swapping ratio definitions, e.g. sin θ = base/hypotenuse (major) · inverting a ratio, e.g. sin θ = hypotenuse/perpendicular (major) · implying the ratios depend on the triangle's size rather than only on θ (major) · applying the right-triangle definitions to a non-right triangle without comment (major) · wrong special-angle values (major) · confusing elevation with depression (major) · applying Pythagoras with the hypotenuse misidentified (major).

## M8 — The quadratic formula and the discriminant *(Grade 10)*
**math10 U1 Quadratic Equations §1.1–1.3, p1–7 (formula derived p5–6) · U2 Theory of Quadratic Equations §2.1, p19–21 (discriminant)**

**Definition as taught (§1.1):** an equation which contains the square of the unknown quantity **but no higher power** is a quadratic equation, or an equation of the **second degree**. General/standard form **ax² + bx + c = 0, where a ≠ 0** and a, b, c are real; a is the coefficient of x², b the coefficient of x, c the constant term. *Remember box:* if a = 0 it reduces to the linear equation bx + c = 0. If b = 0 it is a **pure quadratic** (e.g. x² − 16 = 0).

**The three methods the book names:** factorisation · completing the square · quadratic formula.

**The formula and its derivation (§1.3(i)) — this is the topic's "why" and is an explicit SLO ("derive quadratic formula by using method of completing square"):** divide ax² + bx + c = 0 through by a · shift c/a to the right · add (b/2a)² to both sides · the left side becomes (x + b/2a)² and the right (b² − 4ac)/4a² · take square roots of both sides · rearrange to

**x = ( −b ± √(b² − 4ac) ) / 2a , a ≠ 0**

*Why this matters for the study:* the curriculum standard here is a **derivation**, not a recitation. A model that states the formula and applies it without showing where it comes from is **pedagogically below the textbook standard** — score low on D3, though it is not a factual error. This makes M8 a high-signal topic in the same way M1 is.

**When to use it (§1.3(ii), stated in the book):** the quadratic formula solves **all** quadratic equations, whether or not they can be factorised. Worked: 2 + 9x = 5x² → standard form 5x² − 9x − 2 = 0 → a = 5, b = −9, c = −2 → roots 2 and −1/5.

**Discriminant (§2.1.1):** the nature of the roots depends on the value of **b² − 4ac**, which is called the **discriminant** of the equation (or of the expression ax² + bx + c).

**Nature of the roots (§2.1.3) — the book gives FOUR cases, for rational a, b, c:**
| Discriminant | Nature of roots |
|---|---|
| b² − 4ac > 0 **and a perfect square** | rational (real) and unequal |
| b² − 4ac > 0 **and not a perfect square** | irrational (real) and unequal |
| b² − 4ac = 0 | rational (real) and **equal** |
| b² − 4ac < 0 | **imaginary** (complex conjugates) |

*Grade-standard note (D2 discriminator):* many international treatments collapse this into three cases (two distinct real / one repeated / no real roots). The **perfect-square split** and the use of "imaginary / complex conjugates" rather than "no solution" are the local standard. A three-case answer is not factually wrong but is below the book's treatment.

**Worked examples the book uses:** 2x² − 7x + 1 = 0 → disc 41 · x² − 3x + 3 = 0 → disc −3 · x² − 5x + 5 = 0 → disc 5, positive and not a perfect square → irrational unequal · 2x² − x + 1 = 0 → disc −7 → imaginary unequal · x² + 8x + 16 = 0 → disc 0 → rational equal, verified as x = −4, −4 · 7x² + 8x + 1 = 0 → disc 36 = 6² → rational unequal. Also §2.1.5: finding an unknown k when the nature of the roots is given.

**Topic boundary (same units, beyond the topic label — omitting these is NOT an error and NOT a D2 problem):** equations reducible to quadratic form (ax⁴ + bx² + c, reciprocal, exponential, radical equations and extraneous roots) · cube roots of unity and the properties of ω · relation between roots and coefficients (S = −b/a, P = c/a) · symmetric functions · formation of an equation via x² − Sx + P = 0 · synthetic division · simultaneous equations.

**Errors:** writing the formula with **+b** in the numerator, or **b² + 4ac** under the radical (major — both are printed as distractors in the book's own MCQ) · giving the discriminant as √(b² − 4ac) rather than b² − 4ac (minor) · stating that b² − 4ac < 0 means the equation has **no roots** (major at this grade — the book says imaginary/complex conjugates) · reversing the perfect-square condition, so irrational and rational are swapped (major) · omitting the a ≠ 0 condition when stating the standard form or the formula (minor, but the book prints it every time) · reading off a, b, c without first writing the equation in standard form, producing wrong values (major) · describing the zero-discriminant case as "one root" in a way that denies there are two equal roots (minor — the book's own verification writes x = −4, −4).
